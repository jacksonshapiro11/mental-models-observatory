## The Wild Card

- **Livermore melted diamond and found that a twenty-year argument between experiment and theory was a broken thermometer.** Marius Millot and Jon Eggert's team at Lawrence Livermore, firing samples at the University of Rochester's Omega laser, took X-ray diffraction measurements of carbon through melting at pressures near a terapascal, roughly three times the pressure at the centre of the Earth. The state lasts a billionth of a second. Published in Nature Physics on 13 August, the work closes a disagreement of about 20 percent between measurement and quantum simulation that had stood since the early 2000s: the original temperature readings were off by more than a thousand degrees. The consequence is that fusion capsules can be compressed with gentler initial shocks, which the team estimates is worth roughly three times the energy gain.

- **Across 170 million years, no dinosaur that was not a bird ever got smaller than a large rabbit.** Stephanie Lechki at Princeton and Roger Benson at the American Museum of Natural History put a number on the floor in Evolution on 5 August: about 450 grams. A bee hummingbird weighs 1.75 grams, so the gap is two hundredfold, and roughly 75 percent of living mammal species and 90 percent of living birds sit below a line the dinosaurs never crossed. Physiology does not explain it, since the largest of them exceeded 80 tons. The explanation the authors favour is that early mammals had already taken the small-body niche and kept it. The famous version runs one way, dinosaurs suppressing mammals for a hundred million years. It ran both ways at once, and flight is what let one lineage escape the floor.

- **A model reading ordinary microscope slides guessed an organ's age better than the chemical clocks built to do it.** André Rendeiro's group at the Ludwig Boltzmann Institute for Network Medicine in Vienna trained on 25,712 whole-slide histopathology images covering 40 tissue types from 983 people, in Nature Medicine on 14 August. The resulting tissue clocks estimate age with a mean absolute error of 4.9 years, beating DNA-methylation estimates, and the gaps between a tissue's measured and chronological age predicted eight prevalent diseases including Alzheimer's and stroke across independent cohorts. Every aging clock in wide use is molecular. This one reads architecture, the shape of the tissue rather than its chemistry, so the signal has been sitting in slides hospitals have archived for a century.

## The Signal

**Since June 2025 a ship has had a legal way into the water and almost no legal way out of it, and the scrap value that every vessel valuation treats as a hard floor is the thing that quietly stopped existing.**

Every ship valuation carries a floor: below some freight rate the owner stops trading and sells the hull for steel, and that bid is the bottom of net asset value. The floor assumes a buyer is legally permitted to exist. On 26 June 2025 the IMO's Hong Kong Convention entered into force, and the set of yards permitted to be that buyer began contracting while the fleet needing them grew.

The constraint is authorisation, not price, which is why firmer steel does not reopen the exit. The convention covers ships of 500 gross tons and above on international voyages and requires an authorised yard. South Asian yards took 85 percent of demolished tonnage in the NGO Shipbreaking Platform's 2025 annual list, 321 vessels, and are converting at their own pace: Bangladesh recycled 57 ships in the first half of 2025 against 89 a year earlier. Behind that narrowing sits BIMCO's 2026 count of 15,000-plus vessels approaching end of life.

Analysts read thin demolition as a scrap-price signal that reverses when steel firms. It will not, because the binding input is a certificate. A twenty-five-year-old bulker whose nearest authorised yard is full does not wait, it trades. **If 2026 recycled tonnage falls again while deliveries rise, effective supply runs higher than the orderbook implies exactly when a soft market needs it lower, and the salvage floor under the older owners, Star Bulk (SBLK), Golden Ocean (GOGL), Genco (GNK), Frontline (FRO) and International Seaways (INSW), is a number in a model rather than a bid, while value accrues to whoever is paid for a fleet staying old: Bureau Veritas (BVI.PA), which certifies the yards, and Seatrium (5E2.SI), taking the extra drydockings.** **Watch:** the NGO Shipbreaking Platform's first-half 2026 figures against Bangladesh's 57 ships in H1 2025, and the HKC-authorised facility count on the IMO's GISIS list. If authorisations grow more slowly than the end-of-life fleet, the exit is being removed rather than repriced.

**Roughly 950,000 industrial robots were installed worldwide in four years, most of the growth in China, and that cohort now reaches the age where its motors and gearboxes leave support, putting the largest installed base in history back out to tender.**

The International Federation of Robotics put the world's operational stock at about 1,631,600 robots at end-2015 and 2,589,000 at end-2019, with China taking close to 40 percent of global supply by 2019. Integrators publish a consistent rule of thumb: at about ten years, servo motors and gearboxes fall out of manufacturer support. That is a workshop convention rather than a measured failure distribution, and it is the softest number here, but it puts the largest installation cohort ever recorded into replacement between 2026 and 2029.

A replacement buyer is not the same buyer. A first-time buyer purchases a system: cell design, tooling, programming, training and service, of which the arm is a slice. Ten years later the customer owns the rest and needs one thing, an arm matching the existing cell's footprint, reach, payload and fieldbus. The sale collapses from a system to a component, and components are decided on price and lead time. The switching cost the incumbents' installed-base story rests on was paid a decade ago by a buyer who does not pay it twice.

That Chinese vendors are taking robot share is consensus and sits in sell-side notes. What is not priced is the mechanical clock accelerating it, concentrated where the cohort is largest. **If China's installations hold while the operational stock grows more slowly than installations, the difference is replacement rather than new automation, and the re-tender lands on the incumbents with the biggest Chinese installed base, Fanuc (6954.T), Yaskawa (6506.T) and ABB (ABBN.SW), while the domestic challengers bidding at parity, Estun Automation (002747.SZ) and Shenzhen Inovance (300124.SZ), collect it. Subtract the year-on-year change in operational stock from annual installations; the gap is retirement. If it widens materially above its run rate in the September 2026 edition, the replacement wave has begun and China's installation numbers have stopped measuring new demand.

# ▸ THE TAKE

### Seniority by Retaliation

**A claim's real priority is set by its holder's capacity to impose an immediate, concentrated, attributable cost, not by its contract. The claimant whose only retaliation is slow, diffuse and deniable is the junior one, whatever the indenture says.**

The standard reading of France is a multiple-equilibria one. The debt is sustainable if lenders stay calm and unsustainable if they do not, so a crisis may or may not arrive on investor whim. Hanno Lustig's generational capital structure rejects that model outright. **The French boomers are the senior claimants on the French state**, which makes it a fundamentals problem rather than a coordination one, and bond investors are repricing toward the realisation that they hold the junior claim. The evidence is a policy variable rather than a spread. France's statutory pension age is 62. Macron's 2023 reform to reach 64 by 2030 needed "legislative acrobatics" and months of national strikes. Germany is at 67, the UK 66 heading to 68, Denmark 67 and indexed to life expectancy. France is straining to converge on a number its peers passed years ago, against a pension bill among the largest in the OECD.

Sovereign risk is modelled as a coordination problem, Calvo, Cole-Kehoe, the Diamond-Dybvig bank run transposed onto a state. Every one of those models assumes the borrower is solvent under *some* rate path, so the only question is whether lenders panic. That assumption quietly imports a capital structure: bondholders hold an enforceable contract, pensioners hold a revocable statute, therefore bonds are senior. The revealed ordering is the exact inverse, and corporate bankruptcy documented it forty years ago. Deviations from the absolute priority rule were the norm rather than the exception across the Chapter 11 samples of Franks and Torous (1989), Eberhart, Moore and Roenfeldt (1990) and Weiss (1990), because equity held the leverage of delay and creditors held only a document. Pensioners can strike, vote and end a government inside a news cycle. A bondholder's sole weapon is the yield: slow, diffuse, deniable, and payable by whoever holds office next.

Neither reading draws the consequence. **A junior claimant who cannot be defaulted on is impaired continuously, as price, rather than discretely, as an event.** So the first reading is right that there is no crisis and the second is right that there is no solvency. The adjustment is simply being taken one auction at a time. Thursday's $25 billion US 30-year cleared at 5.216 percent, the highest for the tenor since 2001. Friday, retail sales missed badly and Michigan sentiment printed 51.0 against 55.2, and long yields rose anyway. One might think weak data would pull the long end down. Weak demand no longer pulls the long end down, because what is being repriced is not a growth claim.

**The call, gradable: through 2027-12-31 France enacts no statutory pension age above the 64-by-2030 path already legislated and no nominal benefit cut, with consolidation landing on taxes and discretionary spending instead. If France instead legislates 65-plus or breaks pension indexation, this is wrong about which claim is senior, and French long-end yields should fall against Bunds on the announcement.** The diagnostic transfers to any obligor: before the debt ratio, read the retirement age against the peer median.

**Where this breaks.** Greece is the scope limit and the hardest objection. Pensions were cut there in round after round from 2010 to 2017, precisely because the Troika held the funding tap. Political seniority survives only while a sovereign keeps market access. Lose it and the ordering flips, the implicit claimant is impaired first, hardest and by external instruction. On that reading the framework describes strained-but-funded borrowers and inverts exactly when the question becomes urgent. The second objection is structural, and it attacks the case rather than the mechanism: France is not a monetary sovereign. Its bondholders sit behind a supranational backstop, the ECB's Transmission Protection Instrument and Outright Monetary Transactions before it, with no analogue for a standalone borrower. A small OAT spread may therefore be pricing a central bank's implicit put rather than any claim-priority equilibrium at all, which would make France the wrong worked example even if the mechanism itself is sound. The multiple-equilibria reply is the third: if France really sat in the bad equilibrium, rates would already be very high and the crisis already here, and neither is true. The OAT spread exists but it is small. A small spread is evidence against fundamental insolvency, and multiple equilibria is the right model for an intermediate zone. Fourth, and most damaging to this as a *framework* rather than an observation: Denmark indexed its retirement age to life expectancy, demoting its own senior implicit claimant prospectively and without a crisis. If that move is available to a democracy, the binding constraint is national political contingency rather than a law of political economy, and the whole thing degrades into the unremarkable claim that some countries govern better than others.

# ▸ INNER GAME

*"If a man does not make new acquaintance as he advances through life, he will soon find himself left alone. A man, Sir, should keep his friendship in constant repair."*

— Samuel Johnson, recorded by Boswell

The word doing the work is repair. Not build, not deepen, not treasure. Repair is what you do to a thing that decays on its own whether or not you attend to it.

The mistake is assuming you would feel it decaying. You do not: the feeling does not decay. You can go two years without speaking to someone and the warmth sits where you left it, available on demand. That is memory, and memory is free. The relationship is a different object: it runs on contact, and contact is not free. The two come apart quietly and only one of them sends a signal.

Gerard Manley Hopkins caught the general form of it: the name arrives before you have finished looking. This is that failure aimed at a person instead of a bluebell. You still have the name for them, close friend, and it keeps returning a result long after the thing it points at stopped being maintained.

You know the specific one. The person whose life you follow closely enough to know their news, their move, their kid starting school, and whom you have not spoken to since a date you would have to look up. Following is not contact. It produces the sensation of keeping up while removing the only reason you ever had to reach out, which was not knowing.

Johnson's first sentence is the harder one and nobody quotes it. He is not saying tend what you have. He is saying keep making new ones, because the arithmetic runs against you and maintenance alone loses. Boswell sets the remark beside Johnson's own line in the Dictionary's preface, that most of those whom he had wished to please had sunk into the grave.

**Today's practice: pick the three people whose lives you know best and have not spoken to, and put their names where you are forced to see them weekly. A repeating calendar entry, a note you open each morning. Following tells you everything about them except that you are drifting, so the prompt must come from outside the channel that hid it.**

# ▸ THE MODEL

### Logic & Optimization Under Constraints

In 1945 George Stigler asked what it would cost to keep a man alive. He wrote it up in the *Journal of Farm Economics* as "The Cost of Subsistence": take 77 foods at August 1939 retail prices, take the National Research Council's nine nutritional requirements, find the cheapest combination satisfying all nine. He could not solve it, and said so in one of the more honest sentences in economics: "there does not appear to be any direct method of finding the minimum of a linear function subject to linear conditions." So he narrowed the list by hand, guessed, and published $39.93.

Two years later George Dantzig invented the simplex method, and in 1947 Jack Laderman at the National Bureau of Standards pointed it at Stigler's own data. Nine clerks on hand-operated desk calculators, roughly 120 man-days, nine equations in 77 unknowns. The true minimum was $39.69. Stigler's guess was off by 24 cents, six-tenths of one percent.

The interesting part is what the machine's answer looked like. Five foods. Wheat flour, cabbage, spinach, dried navy beans, and 2.57 pounds of beef liver a year. All nine constraints satisfied, five binding at exactly 100 percent and the rest in enormous surplus, iron at 425 percent. Nutritionally perfect, and something no human being would eat twice. Stigler said it himself: "No one recommends these diets for anyone, let alone everyone."

**Mechanism.** An optimum is not a property of your objective. It is a property of the constraint set, and it sits wherever the binding constraints intersect. Everything you did not write down is, to the solver, free. Not neglected, free: available to be spent to zero in exchange for progress on the thing you did write. Stigler asked for nutrients and cost, got a perfect answer on both, and paid for it out of variety, texture and the will to keep eating. Those were not sacrificed by accident. They were the currency.

**A second domain.** In December 2006 Netherlands Railways replaced a national timetable whose structure dated to 1970, while traffic had roughly doubled underneath it. Rebuilding meant scheduling about 5,500 trains a day, and a typical instance carried around 8,400 events and 70,000 constraints. Mixed-integer programming failed, so the team used constraint programming, because what they needed first was a feasible answer rather than a provably optimal one. Punctuality went from 84.8 percent in 2006 to 87.0 in 2007, a record, on about 40 million euros of additional annual profit rising toward 70. They laid no new track. The binding constraint had been a thirty-six-year-old structure nobody had retested, and the model's most useful output was the list of requested constraints that could not all be true at once.

**Sizing.** Too little and you leave the 1970 timetable running for a generation. Too much and you get the perfect diet. The tradeoff is not effort against result, it is the cost of solving against the cost of specifying, and specifying is the more expensive half. Writing the objective takes an afternoon. Writing down what you refuse to give up takes weeks, and the solver never tells you which one you shortchanged.

**Failure mode.** The model does worse than ignoring it exactly when the constraints are easy to write and the preferences are hard to. Then the optimiser converts the hard ones into slack, efficiently and invisibly. A hiring process optimised for time-to-fill, a hospital for bed turnover, a team for a quarterly number: each finds the true optimum of what it wrote down and pays for it out of what it did not.

**The decision tool: before optimising anything, list the constraints, ask of each whether it is actually binding, then ask the harder question, which is what you left out because it was hard to measure.** Then run the Stigler test on the answer. Would you live inside it for a year? If not, the reason you would not is the constraint you failed to write down, and you have just found it for free.

# ▸ DISCOVERY

### Ask Yourself Twice, and Wait Three Weeks Before You Do

In 2008 Edward Vul and Harold Pashler, then at UC San Diego, gave people eight general-knowledge estimation questions, what percentage of the world's airports lie inside the United States, that sort of thing, and then asked the same people, without warning, to produce a second and different guess for every one. The average of a single person's two guesses beat either guess alone. The effect was larger when the second guess came three weeks later rather than immediately, and a pre-registered replication by Sara Steegen and colleagues in 2014 reproduced both results. What the experiment measures is that a mind does not store a number. It stores a distribution, and every answer you give is one draw from it, carrying error a second draw can partly cancel.

Everyone knows the crowd version, a room full of people guessing the weight of an ox beats almost everyone in the room. The useful finding is the one that sounds impossible: you are already a small crowd, and how much of it you get is set not by how hard you think but by how independent your second look is. Thinking harder about your first answer mostly re-reads the same draw. The three-week delay beat the immediate re-ask because time changes what you retrieve, and a changed retrieval is a new sample rather than a memory of the old one. Which is also why the correction is invisible from inside. Both guesses feel equally right, so introspection cannot tell you which to trust. Only the averaging can.

So when you must commit to a number alone, a forecast, a deadline, a probability, a headcount, write the first one down, put it out of sight, and re-estimate from scratch after a gap, beginning by assuming the first was wrong and asking what would make it so, then take the midpoint. Do not look at the first number while producing the second. The whole benefit lives in the independence and one glance spends it. Test it within a week: log both estimates and the average on three real decisions, and see which was closest when the answer lands. What makes this hard is not the arithmetic. It is that you will never feel it working, because the only instrument available to notice the improvement is the same one generating both guesses. Every other self-improvement you attempt reports back to you. This one asks you to keep running a correction on the authority of a result you cannot personally observe, which is why almost nobody does it, and why the delay is the part people drop first.
