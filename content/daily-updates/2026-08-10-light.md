### Greg Abel Bought the Dip That Never Came

*Stocks closed Friday at a record on their best week in months, and the number underneath that week was 23,000 jobs lost in July. Greg Abel bought back Berkshire stock at a price Warren Buffett spent two decades refusing to pay, and the stock had not fallen a cent to get there. The index ran past it instead. That is the week's shape: prices computed from a comparison rather than from the thing itself. The exception is a Saudi refinery hit twice in thirteen days, where something scarce moved and nothing repriced.*

## ▸ THE UPDATE

**Greg Abel restarted Berkshire's buyback at 1.50 times book, above the ceiling Buffett spent two decades refusing to cross, and the stock never fell to get there.**
Berkshire bought about $4.5 billion of itself last quarter after buying none since May 2024. Buffett deleted the 1.2 times book cap in 2018 and replaced it with his own estimate of the company's worth, so every repurchase is a published opinion. Book value is $348.26 a B share against Friday's $521.80 close. The stock did not get cheap; the index ran past it, and Abel bought a discount his predecessor would not have called one. The objection is that $4.5 billion is 1.2 percent of a cash pile Berkshire refills inside a quarter. Tim Cook's first Apple payout looked small too. What a first one announces is that the rule changed.

**Only 13.6 percent of 1,053 paying developers refused a clearly dangerous command, and the Claude Code mode that catches it 89 percent of the time becomes the default on Friday.**
Simon Willison, whose running log of AI tooling the field reads, published it Saturday. Each developer session had one permission prompt quietly swapped for a plainly harmful command, and roughly six in seven humans approved it. Both caveats are Willison's own: 89 percent leaves 11 percent unblocked, and he wants the third-party injection testing independently confirmed. The finding is not that the machine did well. It is that human review, the control every enterprise AI policy is built on, has been measured at 13.6 percent effectiveness against the exact failure it exists to catch.

**Aramco says it repairs damaged plants six times faster than the industry average, and its Jazan refinery has now been hit twice in thirteen days.**
Houthi forces claimed a drone strike on the 400,000 barrel-a-day plant on Sunday, hours after Saudi Arabia said a fire there was out. Aramco shut the same facility on July 27 with restart targeted for mid-August, and chief executive Amin Nasser made the repair-speed claim on last week's earnings call. Nobody could test it on a repeat strike until now. The honest limits are real: no injuries, the fire out by dawn, the cause undisclosed. But the number that matters is the restart date rather than the damage. If mid-August slips, the spare Saudi capacity the whole crude curve is priced against is a talking point rather than a barrel.

**Every connected product sold into Europe is about to carry a printed expiry date for its security, and the first binding deadline is five weeks away.**
The EU's Cyber Resilience Act applies in full in December 2027, which is why nobody is watching 11 September 2026. From that date any maker selling a connected product into the EU must report an exploited vulnerability within 24 hours, including what is already in the field. The structural half is the support period: five years minimum of handling vulnerabilities, printed on the product. A door lock is sold on a twenty-year field life and booked as a single delivery, and the Act staples a service tail to that sale nobody has reserved against. The fair objection is that European deadlines routinely pass with nobody reserving a euro, because enforcement lags the text by years. The split is already visible: Schneider Electric and Siemens can sell a long support period as a specification, and Logitech and Signify cannot.

## ▸ THE LINE

**The bond market is pricing a decade of inflation at 2.26 percent, below what Wednesday's one month should show.** A hot 8:30 print must move that and September's 42 percent hike odds in opposite directions at once. No crowded side.

**Michael Pettis argues that cutting Chinese rates shrinks the household share of the economy, so rebalancing needs higher rates.** Banks there borrow from households and lend to firms, so a cut moves income between them. Treating Chinese easing as global stimulus has the sign backwards.

**Steve Yegge's team commits code 3.6 times faster than its pipeline can verify it, so continuous integration is a queue rather than a gate.** His 175 commits a day against a thirty-minute build demand 87.5 hours of checking. Verification rather than generation now limits agent-written software.

**Washington is withdrawing the rule that would have forced American medical sterilisation off ethylene oxide, and the switch is happening anyway.** Sotera Health, which owns both technologies, grew its cobalt arm 16.7 percent last quarter against 7.0 at its gas arm. Sunk cost does not reverse.

**The Bitcoin fork that failed last week needs roughly twenty-five years to reach its first difficulty adjustment.** Difficulty re-prices every 2,016 blocks, so relief cannot arrive until the starving chain produces blocks it can no longer produce. Only hashpower closes that, and hashpower is what left.

**There are 518 Russian shadow-fleet tankers the EU and Britain have sanctioned and Washington has not.** Activity drops about 86 percent under an American designation against roughly 50 percent when only Europe acts, because secondary-sanctions fear moves a Greek charterer.

## ▸ THE TAKE

When an index computes a company's weight from its free float, the float stops being a count of shares that can be sold and becomes an instruction to buy. Nasdaq's new schedule counts roughly three times float, and SpaceX's August 6 lockup took its float from 4.9 percent to 11.8. The call: by December's reconstitution SPCX's index weight is at least double its July entry weight, and rises in a month when the share price falls. Where it breaks: Greenwood and Sammon found the abnormal return on an S&P 500 addition fell from 7.4 percent in the 1990s to 0.3 today, because a preannounced order costs less to fill. Any rule computing an entitlement from a quantity a third party controls has handed that party the trigger.

## ▸ INTERESTING THINGS

**The methane the world files under cattle is manufactured by a microbe living inside another microbe.**
Researchers found a structure nobody had seen before inside the single-celled organisms in a cow's rumen. They named it the hydrogenobody, and its job is to pour out hydrogen. Living in those same cells are methanogens, for whom hydrogen is food and methane is breath. A decade of suppression aimed at the methanogen, which is the drain rather than the tap. When a fix keeps needing to be reapplied, you targeted a converter, and you are buying a subscription rather than a solution.

**A supermassive black hole has been found 30,000 light years from its galaxy's centre, with no galaxy visible around it.** Astronomers caught it only because it shredded a passing star, and a detector firing only on disturbance will always undercount.

**Human language diversity peaked between three thousand and one thousand years ago, and the collapse began with states and empires rather than European colonialism.** Tens of thousands of languages became about 7,600, and the most effective monolingual policy in history was never written down as one.

**Laser survey of southwest Amazonia found more than twenty thousand pre-Columbian earthworks, implying up to three million people.** The pristine rainforest that soil and climate baselines are calibrated against is an abandoned garden, and a control group stops being one once somebody was already running the experiment there.

## ▸ THE MEDITATION

*"This Strife is wholesome for men. And potter is angry with potter, and craftsman with craftsman, and beggar is jealous of beggar, and minstrel of minstrel."*
– Hesiod, *Works and Days*

You would assume envy toward a peer is the thing to suppress. Hesiod says the opposite. There is not one Strife. There are two, and the second is worth having.

They arrive wearing the same feeling. The first wants the other person to have less, and is the only ambition you can fully realise while sitting still. The second wants your work to be better.

The test is in his list: potter against potter, beggar against beggar, nobody measuring against a king. Useful Strife fires only against someone near enough that you can see what they did differently, which turns envy into information rather than mood.

Which produces an uncomfortable inventory: the person at your exact level doing your exact job, whose work you stopped reading eight months ago, and whose name now surfaces only inside an explanation of why their approach would not work for you. You made the first Strife comfortable by removing the evidence.

**Today's practice: name the one peer at your level whose work you have been avoiding, spend twenty minutes inside it, and send them one sentence naming the thing they do better. If you looked and sent nothing, the practice did not happen.**

Two ways out, both purchases: destroy your own option to do what the other side counts on, where they can see it, or pay a price only an honest party could afford. He kept three days of rations: he removed the option to wait, not the option to eat.
