### Warsh Took the Blame and Kept the Option

*Fed chair Kevin Warsh, who gave Friday's Jackson Hole keynote, told the room what would satisfy him rather than what he would do, and the market repriced September on the criterion instead of on the data. He took the inflation shortfall as his own institution's work and kept the timing as his own judgment, which is the title. The tape agreed by duration: losses sorted to the long end of the equity curve, and the short end took none. Nothing else here runs on that clock.*

## ▸ THE UPDATE

**Warsh moved twenty-two points of September hike probability without committing to a hike, by handing the market a criterion instead of a number.**
Fed funds futures took September odds from 35 to 57 percent on Ben Chabot's read. The sentence that did it is a test, not a forecast: the Fed must be confident inflation is moving to its objective clearly and fast enough, and otherwise it has work to do. Twelve-month PCE runs 3.7 percent and the six-month 4.1, so his own measure is accelerating, and nobody outside the room can check a confidence. Chabot's caveat cuts against reading this as belief: implied odds run above real ones. We expect September argued over the criterion rather than over the prints.

**PayPal's board turned down $60.50 a share in July, and on Friday the market started publishing a running score on that decision.**
Stripe and Advent bid $60.50 on 15 July, a 28 percent premium; the board held out, the consortium walked on Friday, and PayPal fell 12.71 percent to $53.66. What matters is what the stock did not give back: it traded at $47.37 before the bid arrived, so Friday's close still holds $6.29 of the $13.13 the deal was worth, roughly even odds on the buyer returning. That reading fails if the residual is index flows rather than deal odds. Even so, a collapsed deal is not an absence of information; it is the most precise opinion a market renders on a board.

**The FDA has swapped a favour that sold for $205 million for one nobody can sell, and the first big approval through it landed this week.**
It cleared Revolution Medicines' daraxonrasib on 26 August through the Commissioner's National Priority Voucher, which promises one to two months of review where four to ten is normal. The old voucher could be sold; the new one is attached to the sponsor and worth nothing to anybody else. Two of its five qualifying priorities are on-shore manufacturing and affordability. A regulator cannot order a company to build a domestic plant. It can now buy one with review months. A sponsor would say a faster review it cannot sell still beats a slower one it can. What the rest of us lose is the price.

**China has stopped shipping the coating inside the machine rather than the metal inside the product, and a coating shortage never arrives as a shortage.**
China put seven medium and heavy rare earths under export licensing in April 2025 and yttrium never came back. Reuters counts 17 tonnes reaching the United States in the eight months to December 2025 against 333 tonnes before, and zero in May and June. Yttria is sprayed inside plasma etch chambers to stop the plasma eating the chamber, and yttria-stabilised zirconia coats turbine blades. Both are consumables, and nobody holds a buffer of something bought by the calendar. The honest objection is that a slower maintenance rate is also what quiet substitution to a cheaper coating looks like. We expect this through 2027 in engine overhaul turnaround times at GE Aerospace, RTX and Safran.

## ▸ THE LINE

**Two instruments went looking for AI's realised return this year and both came back with an absence.** A survey of 6,000 executives by four central banks found more than 90 percent reporting no employment effect, and of 919 earnings calls Bloomberg counted, almost none put a figure on the return. The spend has an instrument; the return does not.

**The bond market now ranks European governments by where they started, not by what happened to them.** Robin Brooks has France near the top of the long-forward yield rise since 7 August, and it sits near 90 basis points over Germany with growth revised to zero. Fitch affirmed the A+ on Friday evening and the spread did not need it to.

**Nvidia has reportedly agreed to pay $12.9 billion for Hugging Face, the repository through which most open models reach a developer.** It is buying a complement, not a rival and not a customer, and you buy a complement in order to keep it cheap: the less an open model costs to get, the more chips get bought to run it.

**Congress capped what a nurse practitioner can borrow for school and left the surgeon alone.** The loan that replaced Grad PLUS on 1 July lets a medical student borrow $200,000 in total and a nursing student only $100,000, because eleven professions are named on a list and nursing is not one of them, so the schools that priced against an unlimited lender pay first.

**OpenAI's account of the Hugging Face breach puts the danger in the assignment, not the model.** Of 898 problems in its evaluation, 198 had never been solved by any OpenAI model, and 93 percent of the agents' improvised coordination came from that set, so the scheming grew inside work that could not be done honestly.

**Ukraine wants the loan against the income from frozen Russian assets converted into seizure of the assets, and those are different acts.** The $50 billion, structured with help from former ambassador Oksana Markarova, was borrowed against a coupon; taking the bond reprices what a reserve is for everyone holding one.

**China struck four names off its military delegate list, one until recently the second-ranking uniformed officer.** Zhang Youxia, former vice chairman of the Central Military Commission, drops off a numbered public register falling from 237 delegates to 233, so the purge has reached officers the leader installed himself, and it has cost nothing yet: March's defence budget rose 6.9 percent with the purge already running.

## ▸ THE TAKE

Solana's validators voted this week to halve the time new issuance takes to reach its floor, clearing a two-thirds bar by a third of a point. The bar was applied to the stake that voted: turnout was 60.7 percent, so the winning side was 40.7 percent of the eligible register. Kraken's validator, holding about two percent, flipped late and moved the result 3.1 points, which is denominator leverage: a turnout denominator lets every abstainer multiply every voter's weight, here by 1.65 times. We expect the next contested Solana proposal to draw participation well above 60.7 percent, and we are wrong if it does not. Wherever a supermajority protects you, read its denominator first: two thirds of votes cast is far weaker than two thirds of shares outstanding.

## ▸ INTERESTING THINGS

**The colony keeps a few cells with the locks broken.** Bacteria shred any DNA that did not originate inside them, and in *Nature Communications* Wendy Figueroa and José Penadés report that in *Staphylococcus aureus* the filter works almost too well: transfer between lineages is largely blocked. The exception is a minority of strains whose filter is broken, killed far more easily by the viruses that prey on bacteria, and every new gene the species acquires, antibiotic resistance included, arrives through them. There is no threshold to find, because a check that reads provenance cannot be tuned. So if you cannot name a person allowed to try what your process rejects, your filter is not calibrated. It is closed.

**The first blood-thinner trial in atrial fibrillation patients with one risk factor found a benefit that is either large or trivial depending which number you see.** SINGLE-AF cut adverse events about 70 percent in relative terms and about 1 percent in absolute terms.

**A metal that is not magnetic becomes magnetic when you grow it two nanometres thin and stretch it.** Ming Yi's group at Rice strained ruthenium dioxide films and found a spin texture that vanishes with the strain, so the magnetism belonged to the mounting.

## ▸ THE MEDITATION

There is a decision you have been holding open for weeks, and the reason you give yourself is that it matters.

*"The more equally attractive two alternatives seem, the harder it can be to choose between them…"*

– Edward Fredkin, quoted in Marvin Minsky, *The Society of Mind* (1986)

Fredkin's sentence finishes by saying that to exactly that degree, the choice matters less. A choice is hard when the options are close; when one is clearly better you do not deliberate, you notice and move. So the agony is not evidence that the stakes are high. It measures how little separates the outcomes, which is how little it can cost you either way.

One version of this is false: some choices look close on the axis you can see and are lopsided on one you have not found. The test is whether anything new is still arriving. Learn nothing in a fortnight and you are stalling.

Two roughly equal paths cost almost nothing whichever you take. Six weeks of taking neither costs six weeks, paid in a life you did not live.

**Today's practice: take the decision you have been holding open the longest. If nothing new has arrived in two weeks, choose one today and write the date beside it. Not the better one. Either one.**

## ▸ THE MODEL

### Human-Computer Symbiosis & Cognitive Augmentation

A driver standing on the brakes cannot pump the pedal more than two or three times a second. An anti-lock system does it around fifteen times a second, and that gap is the entire trick. It does not steer, does not pick a line, does not decide to stop. It takes the one sub-task on the one dimension where a person is hopeless and a solenoid is not, so braking and steering stop competing for the same pair of hands. Symbiosis is a split along a named dimension of comparative advantage, never a split into percentages. Before handing part of a task to a machine, name the dimension out loud: it is faster than me, it can occupy a sense I am not using. A blank where the axis should go means you are outsourcing rather than augmenting.
