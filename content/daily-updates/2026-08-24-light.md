## Monday, August 24, 2026

### Two Export Bans and a Bought Vote

*Nothing over the weekend moved because the world ran out of something. Next year's fertilizer bill is being written by a Chinese export licence and a Russian export ban. A lending protocol lost $8.5 million to a vote somebody bought. A British power station was worth attacking because it sat under the legal threshold for reporting it. In each case the price was set by whoever controls the permission, not the material. The honest exception is memory, where the shortage is physical.*

## ▸ THE UPDATE

**Nitrogen fertilizer just posted its steepest annual fall on record, so the story about oil driving your grocery bill has no channel left.**
Urea ended last week at $390 a tonne, down 11.51 percent on the year, and natural gas is most of the cost of making it, so energy pressure would show there first. What is rising is priced by governments: phosphate on Chinese curbs, sulfur up 261.63 percent on a Russian ban. Bans get arbitraged: sulfur is a byproduct nobody chooses to make, so refiners elsewhere can clear it. China's phosphate curbs expire this month, and Beijing's decision matters more to next year's food bill than oil does.

**Two ether bought $8.5 million out of a lending protocol on Sunday, and nothing broke.**
The attacker laundered two ether, bought Term Finance's thinly held governance token until it controlled every vote in four of five dollar vaults, then voted the contents out. PeckShield and CertiK confirmed it. The vaults complied because that is what the code does when a vote passes. Under five thousand dollars in, $8.5 million out, seventeen hundred to one. The fair objection: timelocks and quorum floors exist, and protocols running them lost nothing. That does not explain why the others keep shipping. A protocol's security is the cheaper of two prices, breaking the contract or buying the quorum, and only the first gets audited.

**Nvidia is charging its biggest customers more than 15 percent extra next year rather than absorb an increase a 75 percent gross margin could have swallowed.**
A rack of its top-end servers has priced around $3 million, so 15 percent is several hundred thousand dollars. The cause is memory: contract prices for DRAM, the working memory in every server, rose 90 to 95 percent in one quarter, and Amazon, Google and Meta buy from the same three suppliers Nvidia does. History argues for waiting it out: in 1988 memory more than quadrupled in six months, then collapsed once fab yields arrived. This time the capacity is contracted, not missing, and sold out through 2026. Nvidia is not paying for the memory shortage. The people buying its servers are.

**Europe let every substation already in the ground keep its insulating gas, then voided that permission for any operator who makes one bigger.**
Sulfur hexafluoride is the gas that stops high-voltage switchgear from arcing, and Europe is removing it on a schedule running to 2032. The rule attaches to growth rather than time: installed equipment may keep running, but only while it stays the size it is. A substation that gains a bay for a new data centre counts as new, so an interconnection budgeted as one bay becomes a technology change bought from a short list of vendors. A utility can dodge that by deferring, and queues are full of deferrals, but the data centres are not patient. We expect SF6-free switchgear to win European orders well before 2032, and the projects queued behind them to pay for it in cost and delay.

**Delta will take the share of its fares set by an algorithm from 3 percent to 20 percent, because airlines are exempt from Federal Trade Commission pricing oversight.** The exemption, not the model, decides where individualised pricing spreads next.

**A British power station was reportedly shut down for four days by hackers, and the government's defence is that the plant was too small to be legally required to report it.** One newspaper ties it to Iran and no wire has matched that, but either way the threshold is the attack surface.

## ▸ THE TAKE

A better tool that arrives cheaper gets used by everyone without a decision, so it lifts the floor. One that arrives expensive has to be assigned to particular jobs, and that is a skill most firms lack. In July American businesses bought six percent of their Anthropic tokens from its most capable model; OpenAI's flagship, at half the price, took twenty-five percent. We expect the premium tier to stay under fifteen percent in Ramp's December index, because the binding constraint is price rather than unfamiliarity. Against that, Anthropic's share of US businesses paying for AI rose faster in July than in any month this year. Call it the Dispersion Switch: when an advance arrives dearer rather than cheaper, the gains go to whoever built the skill to place it.

## ▸ INTERESTING THINGS

**Split a group into two piles matching on every attribute at once, and the leftover imbalance has a ceiling almost nobody expected.**
In the early 1980s János Komlós conjectured it never has to exceed one universal constant, however many objects or attributes are involved. Nikhil Bansal and Haotian Jiang moved that ceiling last autumn by tracking a second quantity: nudge one attribute, and how far do all the others move? They then picked the step that disturbed the rest least. What decides whether you land near the bound is not effort per criterion, but whether you are scoring your moves on the wrong axis.

**A humanoid robot ran the hundred metres in 9.39 seconds in Beijing, but the standing high jump is the better number: 2.88 metres against a best of 0.95 a year ago.** Six hundred and sixty-six teams with a fixture list is an industry choosing what to optimise for.

**Swiss watch exports rose 9.6 percent in July, America up 26.5 percent and China down 18.5 percent.** Nobody needs a mechanical watch and the order takes months, which makes it a clean read on who still has surplus.

## ▸ THE MEDITATION

Alexander kept losing his voice on stage in the 1890s and no doctor could say why, so he set up mirrors and watched himself speak.

*"All those who wish to change something in themselves must learn … to inhibit their immediate reaction to any stimulus to gain a desired end."*

– F. M. Alexander, *The Use of the Self* (1932)

The instant he decided to recite, before a sound came out, his head pulled back and his throat shortened. The hoarseness was not happening to him. It was the first thing he did, and trying harder produced a more careful version of the same pull.

What worked was refusing to speak for a moment after deciding to. Just declining to begin. This is why so much self-improvement is effort without movement: the person who resolves to listen better interrupts more attentively. Between deciding and moving is a gap your habit has not claimed.

**Today's practice: pick one thing you do twenty times a day, standing up or reaching for your phone. Put a two-second stop between deciding and moving. Correct nothing, then go and feel what your neck does.**

## ▸ THE MODEL

### Cognitive Biases & Systematic Errors

The textbook proof that intuition is broken was a basketball study: a player who had just scored was no more likely to score again. In 2018 Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo showed the counting itself was biased. Keep only the coin flips that followed a head and you get less than fifty percent, because keeping only those is a selection. Correct for it and the conclusion reverses.

A systematic error is not a mistake in a head. It is a mistake in a procedure, and it repeats in the same direction. A random mistake shrinks as you gather more data; a systematic one gets more confident, because every observation arrives through the same crooked pipe.

**The tool.** Before accepting any comparison, finish this out loud: *what decided which things I am comparing was ______, which pushes the answer ______.* Complete it and you can size the error.
