# LABELING SHEET — 34 units, blind
**One-time owner task. ~25 minutes. Your labels become the instrument's ground truth (amendment 20).**

## What this is, honestly

These 34 units are **two versions of the same brief** — 17 claims, each written twice. They are
shuffled and unlabelled, and the two versions of a claim are always **at least 16 positions apart**.
You will notice that pairs exist. **You are not told which member of a pair is which, and that is the
point** — if you knew, the labels would measure your expectations instead of the prose. Roughly half
the pairs lead with one version and half with the other, so any advantage from meeting a claim twice
cancels in aggregate.

The key is in the repo under a filename that says not to open it yet. Don't.

## How to label

Read **"what it was meant to say,"** then read the unit **once, at normal speed**. Then answer:

- **LANDED** — a smart, busy non-specialist comes away with that claim on one read. Right actor,
  right direction, right rough size, right causal story.
- **DIDN'T** — they don't. Then name what broke, using these five and only these five:

| tag | means |
|---|---|
| **ACTOR** | the reader gets *who or what* wrong — wrong company, wrong agency, wrong party acting |
| **DIRECTION** | the reader gets the sign wrong — up vs down, rose vs fell, won vs lost, more vs less |
| **MAGNITUDE** | the reader gets the size class wrong — millions vs billions, marginal vs decisive |
| **CAUSALITY** | actor, direction and size all survive, but the *why* is wrong, missing, or reversed |
| **NO CLAIM** | nothing usable comes back at all — the reader could not say what the point was |

**Use CAUSALITY only when the first three survive.** It is the residual bucket. The machine grader
already collapses everything into it — 7 of 9 on the synthetic test, including clean actor swaps —
and your labels are the evidence that either confirms or clears that.

**Don't overthink. ~45 seconds each. Your first read is the data; a second read destroys it.**

Edit in place: delete the wrong words on the answer line. Add a note only when the break is interesting.
